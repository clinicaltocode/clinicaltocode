## Post 1: Alert Fatigue

Clinicians override 90%+ of drug interaction alerts.

That's not a people problem. It's a design problem.

We've trained an entire generation of healthcare professionals to ignore the safety nets we built for them — because those safety nets cry wolf hundreds of times a day.

One hospital cut total alert volume by 60% and saw override rates drop from 87% to 34%. More importantly, they caught 3 potential adverse events in the first quarter that the old system would have buried in noise.

The fix isn't more alerts. It's fewer, better ones.

## Post 3: EHR Implementation Mistakes

I've led 3 major EHR implementations.

The lessons were almost never about the technology.

The single biggest predictor of success: Does the project team include clinicians who are empowered to say NO?

Not clinicians who attend meetings and provide input. Clinicians who can veto a configuration decision because it will harm patient care.

If your clinical leads don't have that authority, your implementation is being driven by IT priorities — and it will feel like it.

Other mistakes I see every time:
• Underestimating physician resistance (for rational reasons)
• Building for the spec, not the workflow
• Front-loading training instead of investing in post-go-live support
• Ignoring the night shift

Full breakdown: https://www.clinicaltocode.com/articles/ehr-implementation-mistakes-everyone-makes

## Post 4: FHIR for Clinicians

You've heard "FHIR" in every health IT meeting for the last 5 years.

Here's what it actually means — explained for clinicians, not developers.

When a patient transfers from Hospital A to Hospital B, their records often don't follow them in a useful way. Different EHR systems store data in different formats. One calls it "allergies," another calls it "adverse reactions."

FHIR is the shared language that's supposed to fix this.

But FHIR is a technical standard, not a policy solution. Your hospital can have a perfect FHIR implementation and still not share data with the practice down the street.

If you're a clinical analyst or informaticist, FHIR literacy is becoming essential. You don't need to write code — but you need to understand what's possible.

Read the full explainer: https://www.clinicaltocode.com/articles/fhir-explained-clinicians-interoperability

## Post 5: AI Clinical Notes

We've been piloting ambient AI documentation for 6 months.

The time savings are real: 30-45 fewer minutes per day on documentation.

But here's what nobody wants to talk about:

The AI sometimes gets things wrong. And the errors are subtle enough that a tired physician doing a quick review might miss them.

We found cases where:
• "Patient denies chest pain" became "patient reports chest pain"
• The AI filled in physical exam findings that weren't actually performed
• Family history bled between encounters

A clinical note is a legal document. A hallucinated finding isn't just an inaccuracy — it's a potential malpractice issue.

The technology is coming regardless. Our job is to implement it with the same rigor we apply to any clinical intervention.

Full analysis: https://www.clinicaltocode.com/articles/ai-generated-clinical-notes-promise-peril

## Post 6: Nursing Documentation Burden

In a 12-hour shift, a med-surg nurse might spend 4-5 hours on documentation.

For every hour of direct patient care, there's nearly an equal hour of charting.

When nurses tell me they're thinking about leaving the profession, documentation burden comes up almost as often as staffing and pay.

"I didn't go to nursing school to stare at a screen."

We audited every nursing documentation element at our health system. Nearly 30% fell into "redundant" or "legacy" categories — required by policies that no one could trace to a current requirement.

We eliminated those elements. Nursing satisfaction scores for EHR usability improved by 22 points.

Every unnecessary click is time taken from the bedside. This isn't just an IT problem. It's a workforce crisis.

## Post 8: Sepsis CDS That Actually Works

We reduced sepsis mortality by 18%.

Not by adding more alerts. By embedding intelligence into the workflow nurses were already using.

Instead of a pop-up that fires after criteria are met (when the nurse often already knows something is wrong), we put a running sepsis risk score right next to the vital signs display.

No interruption. Just a quiet indicator that turns yellow or red as the clinical picture evolves.

When it turns red, one button appears: "Initiate Sepsis Bundle."

Results after 12 months:
→ Time to first antibiotic: 4.2 hours → 2.1 hours
→ SEP-1 compliance: 61% → 89%
→ Sepsis mortality: down 18%
→ False positive rate: 12% (vs 34% with pop-up alerts)

We measured whether patients lived. That's the standard every CDS initiative should be held to.

## Post 9: Medication Safety Gaps

CPOE catches the obvious drug interactions.

But the most dangerous medication errors happen in the gaps between what the system checks and what it doesn't.

5 gaps your EHR probably isn't catching:

1. Weight-based dosing without a current weight (23% of patients in our audit had weights >7 days old)
2. Renal dose adjustment that doesn't update when kidney function changes
3. Transition of care blind spots when orders are rewritten during transfers
4. PRN medication accumulation (multiple acetaminophen sources exceeding 4g/day)
5. Look-alike, sound-alike drugs at the point of administration

None of these are unsolvable. But they're not standard, and they're not configured at most hospitals.

## Post 10: Informatics Governance

The best clinical EHR experience I've ever seen was at a community hospital running a mid-tier system with excellent governance.

The worst was at an academic medical center running a top-tier system with no governance at all.

The software matters. But the decision-making structure matters more.

Without governance, you get:
→ 800+ active order sets (fewer than 200 used in the past year)
→ 5 different ways to document a falls risk assessment across 5 units
→ Build team burnout from unprioritized requests

You don't need a committee. You need a gatekeeper. One informaticist who asks three questions before any change enters the queue:

1. Is this clinically necessary?
2. Does something already do this?
3. Will this create work for other departments?

That single checkpoint transforms how your EHR evolves.

## Post 11: CPHIMS Worth It?

Is the CPHIMS certification worth your time and money?

I got mine 2 years into my health IT career. Honest take:

What it DID:
✓ Resume differentiator — 2 of 3 interviews mentioned it
✓ Forced me to learn areas I'd been ignoring (governance, project management, security)
✓ HIMSS membership networking was genuinely valuable

What it DIDN'T:
✗ Meaningful salary bump
✗ Open doors on its own

Best for: Clinical professionals transitioning to health IT who need credibility fast.

Skip if: You're experienced with a strong track record, or in a specialized role where domain-specific certs matter more.

Study for the knowledge. Take the exam for the credential. But invest your real energy in building things, solving problems, and developing relationships.

## Post 12: Telehealth Workflows

We went from 12 telehealth visits per month to 1,200 per week.

We didn't have time to design workflows — we improvised them.

Years later, those improvised workflows are still in place.

What needs fixing:

→ EHR templates designed for in-person visits being used for virtual care
→ Scheduling that doesn't account for different visit durations and tech issues
→ ~20% of patients struggling with the technology
→ No clinical criteria for which visits should be virtual vs. in-person

The technology works. The clinical evidence supports it. What we need now is the operational rigor to make it sustainable.
